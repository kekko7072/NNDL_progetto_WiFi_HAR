\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Starting from a baseline Wi-Fi HAR pipeline evaluated under a strict cross-domain
protocol ($48.2\%$ on unseen environments), we implemented and measured three
strategies one at a time: a supervised-contrastive representation ($53.1\%$,
better on every measurement set, with a t-SNE confirming environment-invariance),
a slower optimizer schedule ($51.3\%$), and multi-antenna soft decision fusion,
which lifts the same weights to $61.5\%$ and overtakes the single-component
ablation of the reference system~\cite{sharp}.

The two effective remedies act on different axes and cost almost nothing: the
contrastive encoder recovers domain robustness on the learning side and yields a
frozen, reusable feature space, while fusion recovers accuracy on the decision
side from receiver diversity the dataset already contains. Combining
them---fusion on top of the frozen probe, with the sliding-window front-end---is
the immediate next step, together with multi-seed runs and the strongest-path
sanitization that still separates us from the full reference pipeline.

\paragraph{What we learned and difficulties encountered}
The strict environment-based split changes the story: modest-looking numbers
($\sim\!50\%$) are the honest cost of never seeing the test environment, which a
random split would have hidden. Two results were counter-intuitive---training
\emph{less} well generalized \emph{better}, and fine-tuning the contrastive
encoder \emph{hurt} by re-overfitting the single environment, which taught us to
trust the frozen-encoder protocol---and the largest gain came from the cheapest
change, once the data pipeline carried a group identifier. The main difficulties
were getting SupCon pre-training to converge (the loss barely moved), the
run-to-run variance of a small model trained on one environment, and the memory
cost of overlapping windows, which forced a pre-allocating data loader before the
$91\%$-overlap front-end could run at all.
