\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Baseline}
On the pooled unseen test set the baseline reaches $\mathbf{48.2\%}$ accuracy
(test loss $1.052$); per set it ranges from $45.5\%$ ($S2$) to $52.3\%$ ($S7$)
(\tab{tab:compare}). Its confusion matrix
(\fig{fig:cm}\,(a)) shows two dominant error modes: \emph{sitting still} is
absorbed by \emph{empty} ($L\!\rightarrow\!E$: $0.76$)---both are near-static and
concentrate energy at zero Doppler velocity---and \emph{running}/\emph{walking}
are heavily confused with each other ($W\!\rightarrow\!R$: $0.39$,
$R\!\rightarrow\!W$: $0.28$).

\subsection{Contrastive model}
Fitting the linear probe on the frozen SupCon encoder raises overall accuracy to
$\mathbf{53.1\%}$ (validation $58.3\%$), an improvement of $+4.9$ points over the
baseline that holds on \emph{every} measurement set
(\tab{tab:compare}): the largest gains are on $S3$
($+8.3$, unseen person) and $S7$ ($+5.9$, all-new). Its confusion matrix
(\fig{fig:cm}\,(b)) improves \emph{running} recall markedly
($0.53\!\rightarrow\!0.56$ and, more importantly, fewer cross-class leaks) while
the low-motion $L\!\rightarrow\!E$ confusion softens ($0.76\!\rightarrow\!0.52$).

The t-SNE projection (\fig{fig:tsne}) is the most informative result.
Colored by \emph{environment} (right), the points are thoroughly mixed---the
SupCon objective \emph{did} achieve its design goal of an environment-invariant
representation. Colored by \emph{activity} (left), the space splits cleanly into
a low-motion lobe (\emph{empty}, \emph{sitting}) and a high-motion lobe
(\emph{jumping}, \emph{running}, \emph{walking}), but the individual activities
\emph{within} each lobe remain partly entangled. Contrastive learning thus
removed the \emph{domain} nuisance but exposed a harder, intrinsic limit: a
single-antenna $\approx\!2$\,s Doppler window does not fully separate the
activities, whichever way it is encoded. Consistently, the SupCon loss decreased
only from $\sim\!4.6$ to $\sim\!4.4$ with a noisy validation curve---the
pre-training did not fully converge.

Interestingly, \emph{end-to-end fine-tuning} of the encoder \emph{hurts}: it
drops back to $48.4\%$, essentially the baseline level. Freezing the
contrastively-pretrained encoder is what generalizes; fine-tuning lets the
network re-overfit the single training environment. Two caveats temper the
headline gain: the small baseline is high-variance across runs (a separate run
reached $\sim\!53\%$), so the $+4.9$-point improvement is best read as
indicative and would ideally be confirmed over several seeds; and, as
\tab{tab:compare} shows, both models stay far below full SHARP.

\subsection{Comparison with the SHARP paper}
\tab{tab:compare} places our results next to the SHARP paper~\cite{sharp}.
Two SHARP columns are shown: the \emph{full} system (strongest-path phase
sanitization $+$ 4-antenna decision fusion) and an \emph{ablation} that replaces
the strongest-path sanitization with the standard reference-antenna method,
feeding the \emph{same} CNN (\cite{sharp}, Table~4). Full SHARP stays above
$95\%$ on every unseen set. Our contrastive model ($53.1\%$) reaches roughly the
level of SHARP's single-component ablation ($\sim\!53\%$ mean), and our baseline
sits just below it. The $\sim\!45$-point gap to full SHARP is thus explained by
the two ingredients we do not implement (\secref{sec:discussion}), not by a bug
in either model.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why both models are far from SHARP}
Full SHARP owes its environment independence to two mechanisms we lack.
\emph{(i) Multi-antenna decision fusion:} SHARP runs the single-antenna
classifier on each of the four antennas and combines the four decisions; input
concatenation is markedly worse (e.g.\ $81.7\%\!\rightarrow\!96.6\%$ on $S5$) and
accuracy grows monotonically with the number of fused antennas~\cite{sharp}, so
our single-antenna model sits at the low end. \emph{(ii) Robust phase
sanitization:} SHARP uses the \emph{strongest} multipath component as phase
reference; its own ablation shows the standard reference-antenna method makes the
\emph{same} CNN collapse from $>95\%$ to $39$--$60\%$ (\tab{tab:compare}, last
column)---right where our models land. Two further factors matter: we use
\emph{non-overlapping} windows (SHARP slides the window, yielding far more
samples) and train on a single small environment, making the baseline
high-variance.

\paragraph{What the contrastive extension achieves}
The SupCon stage attacks domain shift from the \emph{representation} side rather
than the signal-processing side: it \emph{learns} an embedding in which
same-activity windows cluster across domains, aided by the domain-randomization
augmentations. The t-SNE (\fig{fig:tsne}) confirms this worked---the space is
environment-invariant---and the linear probe improves accuracy on every set. Yet
overall accuracy is still about half that of full SHARP, because the residual
errors are \emph{activity} confusions (walking vs.\ running, sitting vs.\ empty),
not \emph{domain} shift. The two ideas are thus \emph{complementary}: contrastive
learning handles the domain axis, while separating the activities most likely
requires the multi-antenna information that SHARP's fusion exploits. Combining the
frozen SupCon encoder (which generalizes far better than fine-tuning) with antenna
fusion is the most promising direction left open.
