\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers come from the stored notebook outputs, one run per configuration,
under the same protocol: train on $S1$, test on the unseen $S2$--$S7$.
\tab{tab:compare} collects them per measurement set, \fig{fig:perset} plots the
same data, and \tab{tab:versions} maps each column to the change that produced
it.

\subsection{Baseline}
On the pooled unseen test set the baseline reaches $\mathbf{48.2\%}$ accuracy
(test loss $1.052$), ranging from $45.5\%$ ($S2$) to $52.3\%$ ($S7$). Its
confusion matrix (\fig{fig:cm}\,(a)) shows two dominant error modes:
\emph{sitting still} is absorbed by \emph{empty} ($L\!\rightarrow\!E$:
$0.76$)---both are near-static and concentrate energy at zero Doppler
velocity---and \emph{running}/\emph{walking} are heavily confused with each other
($W\!\rightarrow\!R$: $0.39$, $R\!\rightarrow\!W$: $0.28$).

\subsection{Contrastive representation}
The linear probe on the frozen SupCon encoder raises overall accuracy to
$\mathbf{53.1\%}$ (validation $58.3\%$): $+4.9$ points, and an improvement on
\emph{every} set, the largest on $S3$ ($+8.3$, unseen person) and $S7$ ($+5.9$,
all conditions new). Its confusion matrix (\fig{fig:cm}\,(b)) sharpens
\emph{running} and softens the $L\!\rightarrow\!E$ confusion
($0.76\!\rightarrow\!0.52$).

The t-SNE projection (\fig{fig:tsne}) is the most informative diagnostic.
Colored by \emph{environment} (right) the points are thoroughly mixed---the
SupCon objective \emph{did} reach its design goal of an environment-invariant
representation. Colored by \emph{activity} (left) the space splits into a
low-motion and a high-motion lobe, but the activities \emph{within} each lobe
stay partly entangled: contrastive learning removed the \emph{domain} nuisance
and exposed a harder, intrinsic limit---a single-antenna $\approx\!2$\,s window
does not fully separate the activities, whichever way it is encoded.
Consistently, the SupCon loss fell only from $\sim\!4.6$ to $\sim\!4.4$ with a
noisy validation curve. \emph{End-to-end fine-tuning} then \emph{hurts},
dropping back to $48.4\%$: freezing the pre-trained encoder is what generalizes,
while fine-tuning re-overfits the single training environment.

\subsection{Optimizer schedule}
The slow schedule changes only the learning rate, the patience and the epoch
budget (\tab{tab:schedule}), so the two runs isolate that factor. Slowing
training down \emph{improves} cross-domain accuracy from $48.2\%$ to $51.3\%$
($+3.1$ points, five sets out of six) even though the model fits its own training
data \emph{worse} ($0.575$ vs.\ $0.601$, \fig{fig:curves}). With a single
training environment the two objectives conflict: an epoch spent fitting $S1$
better is also an epoch spent memorizing its room, person and day. It also
tempers the previous subsection---$+3.1$ of the contrastive model's $+4.9$ points
can be bought with an optimizer setting alone, so single-run gains of a few
points here are fragile.

\subsection{Multi-antenna decision fusion}
Fusion is the largest single improvement we measured. Averaging the four
per-antenna probability vectors (\eq{eq:fusion}) lifts the \emph{same} model from
$51.3\%$ to $\mathbf{61.5\%}$: $+10.2$ points overall, with per-set gains between
$+6.4$ ($S6$) and $+17.4$ ($S7$) and no set left behind (\fig{fig:perset}). It
costs one softmax and one group-average at inference---no retraining, no extra
parameters.

The mechanism is visible in \fig{fig:cm}\,(c--d): fusion does not repair a
broken class, it sharpens decisions the single-antenna model already leaned
towards (\emph{empty} $0.82\!\rightarrow\!0.89$, \emph{jumping}
$0.50\!\rightarrow\!0.54$, \emph{running} $0.46\!\rightarrow\!0.61$,
\emph{walking} $0.41\!\rightarrow\!0.57$). The antennas observe the same motion
through different multipath, so their errors are only partially correlated and
averaging cancels part of them. The exception is the low-motion pair: $L$ stays
confused with $E$ ($0.54$ of its windows) because all four antennas agree on the
same wrong answer---with almost no motion, extra spatial views add no
information, and that residual error belongs to the $2$\,s window
representation, not to the decision rule.

\subsection{Sliding-window front-end}
\label{sec:sliding}
This is the one change we cannot yet quantify: the notebook that adopts it as
the default ships with its outputs cleared, and the $50\%$-overlap prototype was
never executed end-to-end. The code does establish the mechanism and its cost:
the same recordings yield $\approx\!10\times$ more training windows, at
$\approx\!20$\,GB of resident memory and an epoch time an order of magnitude
longer---exactly why the loader had to allocate once instead of concatenating.
Whether those extra, strongly correlated windows convert into accuracy is left
open; SHARP's own use of stride~$30$ suggests they help, but we report the change
as implemented, not as measured.

\subsection{Comparison with the SHARP paper}
\tab{tab:compare} places our results next to the SHARP paper~\cite{sharp}: the
\emph{full} system (strongest-path sanitization $+$ 4-antenna fusion) and an
\emph{ablation} that swaps the strongest-path sanitization for the standard
reference-antenna method, feeding the \emph{same} CNN (\cite{sharp}, Table~4).
Full SHARP stays above $95\%$ everywhere. Our contrastive model ($53.1\%$)
reaches the level of that ablation ($\sim\!53\%$ mean); the fused model
($61.5\%$) overtakes it and, on the blocked-line-of-sight sets $S4$ and $S5$
where the ablation collapses to $43.5\%$ and $39.2\%$, beats it by $14$ to $18$
points. The residual $\sim\!35$-point gap is therefore concentrated in the one
ingredient we do not implement---the strongest-path phase sanitization---and no
longer in the fusion step. The acquisition campaigns behind the dataset also
spanned nearly two years (\cite{sharp}, Sec.~5.2), and that time diversity is
itself part of what the reference results build on.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What each change bought}
Ranked by measured effect, decision fusion ($+10.2$ points) dominates the
contrastive representation ($+4.9$) and the optimizer schedule ($+3.1$). The
ordering is itself a finding: the cheapest change---a few lines of
post-processing over information already in the dataset---beat the one that cost
the most design effort. It also matches SHARP's ablation, where accuracy grows
monotonically with the number of fused antennas~\cite{sharp}.

\paragraph{Why we are still far from SHARP}
The gap is now essentially one component. SHARP uses the \emph{strongest}
multipath component as phase reference, and its own ablation shows the standard
reference-antenna method makes the \emph{same} CNN collapse from $>95\%$ to
$39$--$60\%$ (\tab{tab:compare}, last column)---the band our models occupy, ours
at its top. That is consistent with having reproduced the decision-level half of
SHARP's robustness and none of its signal-processing half. A second, smaller
factor is that training on one small environment makes every number
high-variance.

\paragraph{The two remedies are complementary}
The SupCon stage attacks domain shift on the \emph{representation} axis and, as
the t-SNE shows, succeeds there; its residual errors are \emph{activity}
confusions, exactly what fusion attacks. The obvious experiment left undone is
\eq{eq:fusion} on top of the frozen SupCon probe with the sliding-window
front-end: all three components exist in the released code, but no single run
combines them.
